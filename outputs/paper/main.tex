% ABSTRACT  [Yatin]
% ================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Rare autoimmune conditions affect millions of people worldwide, yet over 95\% of patients with such conditions lack any FDA-approved disease-modifying therapy. Although drug repurposing presents a faster and more cost-effective alternative to traditional drug development, computational methods frequently fail under conditions of severe sparsity in curated drug--disease associations within biomedical knowledge graphs. This paper investigates the effectiveness of path-based graph neural architectures combined with cross-disease transfer learning for zero-shot drug candidate identification in rare autoimmune diseases.

We present BioPathNet++, a framework that enriches the Drug Repurposing Knowledge Graph (DRKG) with Orphanet and HPO annotations (84,712 nodes; 1,906,058 edges) and trains a Neural Bellman--Ford network on four data-rich autoimmune diseases to internalize shared immune signaling representations. Through a systematic seven-layer evaluation across six zero-shot autoimmune conditions, we demonstrate that: (1)~shared immune pathway representations transfer effectively across disease boundaries without target-specific training, achieving a test AUPRC of 0.935 and per-disease zero-shot AUPRC from 0.871 to 1.000; (2)~pretrained biomedical language model features (BioBERT) are the dominant driver of predictive performance, contributing a $-$0.332 AUPRC drop when removed; (3)~few-shot fine-tuning provides negligible improvement over zero-shot transfer, indicating the pretrained representations already capture near-optimal autoimmune mechanism coverage; and (4)~100\% of top-ranked candidates across all six diseases correspond to compounds already under clinical investigation on ClinicalTrials.gov, independently validating the biological plausibility of the architecture's learned representations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Drug repurposing aims at discovering new therapeutic applications for existing approved drugs. Compared to classical drug discovery pipelines, which can require over a decade of development and billions of dollars~\cite{drkg}, repurposing is considerably faster and more affordable since the safety profiles of candidate drugs are already established. This advantage is particularly valuable in the context of autoimmune diseases, where large therapeutic gaps persist across many patient populations.

Autoimmune diseases span a wide spectrum, from well-studied conditions such as Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA), Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), Multiple Sclerosis (MS), and Crohn's Disease, to rare and poorly characterized disorders including Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) and Systemic Sclerosis. Common autoimmune diseases have hundreds of curated drug--disease associations in databases such as DRKG, whereas rare conditions may have fewer than 20 known treatment links. This severe imbalance makes it extremely difficult for standard supervised machine learning models to generalize effectively to rare diseases.

Biomedical knowledge graphs integrate heterogeneous evidence from drugs, diseases, genes, pathways, and phenotypes into a unified relational structure. Graph neural networks have become widely used for link prediction over such graphs~\cite{distmult}, but neighborhood-based aggregation strategies commonly fail when a disease node has very few connections. They also tend to produce black-box predictions that are difficult to interpret in clinical settings.

Path-based reasoning offers a more principled alternative. By explicitly modeling multi-hop biological routes between drugs and diseases, it becomes possible to learn mechanistic explanations alongside predictions~\cite{nbfnet}. A central question in rare disease drug repurposing is whether shared immune mechanisms---TNF-$\alpha$ signaling, JAK--STAT modulation, cytokine dysregulation---are sufficiently conserved across autoimmune conditions that a model trained on data-rich diseases can generalize to rare, unseen conditions without any target-specific supervision. BioPathNet++ is designed to investigate this question through a combination of knowledge graph enrichment, immune-specific path reasoning, and multi-disease pretraining.

The key contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An empirical investigation demonstrating that path-based graph neural architectures with cross-disease transfer learning can achieve zero-shot AUPRC $\geq 0.87$ across six autoimmune diseases, validating the hypothesis that shared immune representations are transferable across disease boundaries.
    \item A knowledge graph enrichment strategy that augments DRKG with Orphanet and HPO annotations, expanding the graph by 33\% to 84,712 nodes and 1.9 million edges, and an analysis of its impact on rare disease connectivity.
    \item A finding that pretrained biomedical language model features (BioBERT/ChemBERTa) contribute the dominant share of discriminative signal ($-$0.332 AUPRC when removed), outweighing graph-structural components by an order of magnitude.
    \item A comprehensive seven-layer validation framework---benchmark evaluation, per-disease transfer, few-shot comparison, literature validation, immune pathway analysis, clinical trial concordance, and ablation study---providing systematic evidence for the viability of this architectural approach.
\end{itemize}

\section{Existing Work}

Existing work on rare autoimmune drug repurposing can broadly be categorized into two groups: methods that support the need for path-based reasoning and transfer learning, and methods that rely on neighborhood aggregation or embedding-based similarity.

In the first category, RD$^2$-KG~\cite{rd2kg} demonstrated that augmenting standard biomedical knowledge graphs with Orphanet and HPO data yields a 20 to 30 percent improvement in rare disease drug prediction. However, it still relies on node-level embeddings and does not perform explicit multi-hop mechanistic reasoning. The original BioPathNet~\cite{nbfnet} used the Neural Bellman--Ford Network to create pairwise drug--disease representations through all possible relational paths, achieving improved interpretability over standard GNN baselines. Its limitation is that it was not designed specifically for autoimmune diseases and does not investigate disease-level transfer learning. TxGNN~\cite{txgnn} trained a heterogeneous GNN with disease--disease metric learning to enable zero-shot prediction for unseen diseases, achieving approximately 49 percent improvement in zero-shot settings. It is, however, a general-purpose model, computationally demanding, and does not exploit immune-mechanism-specific reasoning.

In the second category, HGTDR~\cite{hgtdr} used heterogeneous graph transformers with type-specific attention for drug--disease link prediction, showing around 9 percent improvement over GCN baselines on new diseases. The attention weights in this model lack explicit biological meaning, and the approach remains fundamentally neighborhood-driven, limiting its interpretability for rare autoimmune conditions. DREAMwalk~\cite{dreamwalk} applied semantic-guided random walks and Skip-Gram embeddings to infer drug--disease similarity, reducing hub-gene bias and improving association prediction accuracy. However, embedding similarity does not support causal or mechanistic reasoning, and the method includes no transfer learning component, making it insufficient under extreme data sparsity.

\section{Related Work}

The broader landscape of knowledge-graph-based drug repurposing spans knowledge graph embedding methods, graph neural networks, transformer-based architectures, and clinical surveys focused on autoimmune diseases. Though each of these avenues has contributed meaningfully to computational drug discovery, they collectively illustrate persistent limitations in interpretability, rare disease modeling, and cross-disease transfer.

Early knowledge graph embedding approaches such as DistMult~\cite{distmult} modeled drug--disease associations through bilinear scoring of entity and relation vectors. While computationally efficient, these methods capture only pairwise co-occurrence statistics and are inherently weak at modeling the multi-hop biological pathways that mechanistically link drugs to diseases. Literature-based KGE methods improved on this by incorporating uncertainty modeling, but remained limited to edges extractable from published literature, restricting coverage for rare diseases.

Graph attention networks introduced attention-based neighborhood aggregation, allowing models to weight neighbor contributions differently. These were, however, designed as general-purpose architectures and are not tailored to the semantics of biomedical graphs. Adaptive graph convolutional networks extended this with learnable adjacency structures, but evaluations focused on single-disease settings and did not address transfer across disease categories.

While recent clinical surveys on autoimmune drug repurposing have provided important biological context through their analysis of treatment overlaps and mechanistic similarities across autoimmune conditions, these surveys lack predictive computational frameworks and rely entirely on manually curated evidence.

DREAMwalk~\cite{dreamwalk} addressed hub-gene bias through semantic-guided random walks and, while it improved association prediction accuracy, it produces similarity-based scores rather than mechanistic explanations and does not support transfer to unseen diseases. Collectively, these works motivate the design of BioPathNet++, which specifically targets the combination of multi-hop immune pathway reasoning, rare disease enrichment, and zero-shot cross-disease transfer that existing methods individually lack.

\section{Data Exploration and Knowledge Graph Construction}

Three categories of data sources are central to this work. First, \textbf{DRKG}~\cite{drkg} consolidates drug--gene--disease associations from DrugBank, Hetionet, STRING, and DGIdb into a single multi-relational graph containing 97,238 entities and 5,874,261 triples across 107 relation types. While comprehensive in scope, DRKG exhibits severe connectivity imbalance: Rheumatoid Arthritis has 1,695 drug-associated links whereas Sj\"{o}gren's Syndrome has only 14, Systemic Sclerosis has 173, and Primary Biliary Cholangitis has 207, with limited mechanistic annotation relative to the training diseases.

To address this sparsity, we enrich DRKG with two rare-disease resources. \textbf{Orphanet}~\cite{orphanet} contributes approximately 150,000 disease--gene association triples across 9,000+ rare diseases. \textbf{HPO}~\cite{hpo} adds approximately 250,000 disease--phenotype annotation triples covering 16,000+ clinical phenotype terms. The resulting enriched Biological Regulatory Graph (BRG) expands the base DRKG by 33\%, yielding 84,712 nodes and 1,906,058 edges, substantially improving connectivity around the target rare-disease nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, Phase~1).

\subsection{Disease Coverage and Evaluation Splits}

The autoimmune subgraph spans nine diseases in total. Four data-rich diseases---Rheumatoid Arthritis, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Multiple Sclerosis, and Crohn's Disease---are used for joint pretraining. Two diseases, Primary Biliary Cholangitis and Systemic Sclerosis, are the primary rare-disease targets and are held out entirely from training for zero-shot evaluation. To further stress-test cross-disease transfer, the framework is additionally evaluated in a zero-shot setting on three more autoimmune conditions: Sj\"{o}gren's Syndrome, Autoimmune Hepatitis, and Myasthenia Gravis.

Crohn's Disease occupies a special role in this expanded evaluation: it is used during joint pretraining as one of the four data-rich diseases, but is additionally scored under a leave-one-disease-out (LODO) protocol, in which a variant of the model pretrained without Crohn's-specific supervision is evaluated zero-shot on its held-out Crohn's links. This LODO run is reported alongside the true zero-shot diseases in Section~VI-B to test whether the transfer mechanism generalizes even for a disease the base model has otherwise seen, and should be read as a robustness check rather than a claim that Crohn's is data-sparse.

\subsection{GNN-Based Link Prediction}

Path-based drug repurposing on biomedical KGs involves four core technical components. GNN-based link prediction aggregates neighborhood information across $K$ message-passing layers via:

\begin{equation}
h_v^{(k)} = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} W_r^{(k)} \cdot \left[h_u^{(k-1)} \,\|\, e_r\right]\right)
\end{equation}

where $W_r^{(k)}$ is a relation-specific weight matrix, $e_r$ is the relation embedding, and $\sigma$ is ReLU activation. This formulation enables the model to learn distinct transformations for each relation type in the biomedical knowledge graph, capturing the semantic differences between ``inhibits,'' ``associates,'' and ``treats'' edges.

\subsection{Neural Bellman--Ford Network (NBFNet)}

NBFNet~\cite{nbfnet} extends standard message passing by initializing a query-specific boundary representation at the source drug node and propagating it along all relational paths:

\begin{equation}
h_v^{(0)} = \mathbb{1}[v = s] \cdot e_{\text{query}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
h_v^{(k)} = \bigoplus_{(u,r,v) \in \mathcal{E}} \phi\!\left(h_u^{(k-1)}, e_r\right)
\end{equation}

where $\phi$ is a DistMult message function $h_u \odot e_r$ and $\bigoplus$ is sum aggregation. This formulation allows the target disease node to accumulate multi-hop path evidence without explicit path enumeration, yielding interpretable path-level attention weights. We use 4 NBFNet layers (exploring up to 4-hop paths) with hidden dimension 512 and a 2-hop subgraph sampling strategy for memory efficiency. The 4-hop path budget is sufficient to cover the dominant drug--gene--pathway--disease mechanistic chains in autoimmune biology.

\subsection{Immune-Specific Metapath Reasoning}

Seven immune-specific metapaths constrain inference to typed, biologically meaningful sequences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drug $\to$ \textit{inhibits} $\to$ TNF-$\alpha$ $\to$ \textit{regulates} $\to$ Disease (TNF-$\alpha$ inhibition)
    \item Drug $\to$ \textit{modulates} $\to$ JAK1/2 $\to$ \textit{activates} $\to$ STAT3 $\to$ Disease (JAK-STAT)
    \item Drug $\to$ \textit{blocks} $\to$ IL-6 $\to$ \textit{triggers} $\to$ Disease (IL-6 blockade)
    \item Drug $\to$ \textit{targets} $\to$ CD20/CD4 $\to$ \textit{depletes} $\to$ Disease (T/B-cell targeting)
    \item Drug $\to$ Gene $\to$ Pathway $\to$ Disease (pathway intervention)
    \item Drug $\to$ Gene $\to$ Gene $\to$ Disease (gene--gene interaction)
    \item Drug $\to$ Phenotype $\to$ Disease (phenotype-based inference)
\end{itemize}

Each metapath type is encoded by a dedicated GRU module that processes the variable-length sequence of node representations along the path. Multi-head attention (8 heads) then learns the relative importance of each metapath for a given drug--disease pair, allowing the model to weight TNF-$\alpha$ inhibition paths more heavily for diseases driven by TNF signaling while emphasizing JAK-STAT paths for others.

\subsection{Multi-Disease Transfer Learning}

The model is pretrained jointly on four data-rich training diseases---Rheumatoid Arthritis, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, Multiple Sclerosis, and Crohn's Disease---using binary cross-entropy loss with hard negatives from RepoDB failed clinical trials (ratio 1:1:2 positive:hard:easy). The learned parameters $\theta_{\text{autoimmune}}$ encode shared mechanisms including TNF-$\alpha$ inhibition, JAK-STAT modulation, and B/T-cell targeting. These are then applied zero-shot to the six evaluated target diseases (Section~VI), without any fine-tuning. We additionally compare with 5-shot and 10-shot fine-tuning that adapts only the prediction head and fusion layers.

The transfer learning hypothesis rests on the observation that autoimmune diseases share conserved immune dysregulation pathways. A model that learns to recognize drug--pathway--disease chains in data-rich conditions should generalize to rare conditions that exhibit similar pathway involvement, even when the specific drug--disease edges have never been observed during training.

\subsection{Architecture Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the complete four-phase pipeline. The model uses hidden dimension 512, 4 NBFNet layers, 7 metapaths, 8 attention heads, dropout 0.2, AdamW optimizer with learning rate $5 \times 10^{-5}$, mixed-precision training, and was trained for 100 epochs on a GPU with 24\,GB memory. The best checkpoint is selected by validation AUPRC (0.918 at epoch 89). Total model size is approximately 7 million parameters.

\section{Results}

We evaluate BioPathNet++ through a seven-layer validation framework: (1)~benchmark performance on the held-out test set, (2)~per-disease zero-shot transfer across six autoimmune conditions, (3)~few-shot fine-tuning comparison, (4)~PubMed literature validation, (5)~immune pathway analysis, (6)~clinical trial concordance, and (7)~an ablation study isolating the contribution of each architectural component.

\subsection{Benchmark Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:sota} compares BioPathNet++ against standard baselines on the held-out test set. BioPathNet++ achieves a test AUPRC of 0.935 and AUROC of 0.941, surpassing TxGNN on both metrics. Test performance exceeds validation performance (AUPRC 0.935 vs.\ 0.918), indicating strong generalization rather than overfitting. Rank-based metrics are markedly lower (MRR 0.021, Hit@10 0.035, Hit@20 0.069) because the evaluation ranks each true drug--disease pair against the full candidate pool rather than a small negative set; Precision@10 of 1.000 confirms that, despite low absolute rank scores, the small set of top-ranked candidates the model surfaces is reliably correct.

\subsection{Clinical Trials Validation}

As a complementary, orthogonal check to literature validation, we cross-reference the same 120 predictions against registered studies on ClinicalTrials.gov. Every one of the six evaluated diseases achieves a 100\% match rate, with 1,575 unique clinical trials identified in total across all predicted drug--disease pairs. This indicates that the vast majority of BioPathNet++'s top-ranked candidates, whether previously known treatments or novel predictions, correspond to compounds that have already entered some stage of clinical investigation for the corresponding condition---a useful translational signal even where mechanistic pathway annotation is still incomplete.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates the contribution of each architectural component by removing it from the full model and re-measuring test AUPRC/AUROC. Removing BioBERT node-feature initialization causes by far the largest degradation ($-0.332$ AUPRC, $-0.324$ AUROC), confirming that pretrained biomedical language embeddings---rather than the graph structure alone---carry the majority of the discriminative signal for rare, sparsely-connected diseases. \section{Discussion}

The experimental evaluation of BioPathNet++ yields several findings with implications for the design of computational drug repurposing systems targeting rare autoimmune diseases.

\textbf{Finding 1: Shared immune representations transfer effectively under extreme data sparsity.} The framework achieves AUPRC $\geq 0.87$ across all six evaluated diseases without receiving any target-specific training supervision. This confirms that shared immune signaling pathways---such as TNF-$\alpha$ regulation, JAK--STAT modulation, and cytokine cascade control---are sufficiently conserved across autoimmune conditions to enable effective cross-disease generalization. Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) exemplifies this transfer, benefiting from the TNF and NF-$\kappa$B pathway representations internalized during pretraining, which yields a 95.0\% immune pathway match rate (19/20 top predictions). The model's top-4 PBC predictions---Thalidomide (8.464), Ursodeoxycholic Acid (8.445), Fenofibrate (8.435), and Prednisolone (8.427)---are all clinically established therapies surfaced purely through graph-based reasoning without any disease-specific training examples.

\textbf{Finding 2: Pretrained language model features dominate graph-structural contributions.} The ablation study reveals that BioBERT node-feature initialization contributes an order of magnitude more discriminative signal ($-$0.332 AUPRC, $-$0.324 AUROC) than any graph-structural component. Removing the fusion mechanism causes a secondary but still notable drop ($-$0.029 AUPRC), while removing metapaths, BRG, or collapsing to a single NBFNet layer leaves performance effectively unchanged. This suggests that for sparsely connected rare disease nodes, the semantic information encoded in pretrained biomedical language model embeddings is more informative than the topological structure of the knowledge graph itself. The graph-structural components (metapaths, multi-hop reasoning) primarily contribute interpretability rather than raw predictive accuracy---an important architectural insight for future system design.

\textbf{Finding 3: Zero-shot transfer is already near-optimal.} A key empirical result is that zero-shot inference consistently matches or outperforms few-shot fine-tuning (5-shot and 10-shot adaptation of the prediction head and fusion layers). Across target diseases, fine-tuning produces negligible or slightly negative performance changes (e.g., Systemic Sclerosis AUPRC moves from 0.871 in zero-shot to 0.865 in 5-shot/10-shot; PBC remains nearly flat from 0.949 to 0.945/0.952). This indicates that joint pretraining on data-rich autoimmune diseases effectively saturates the transferable immune mechanism manifold. Under extreme data sparsity, small target support sets fail to provide additional gradient signal and risk slight overfitting or distortion of the pretrained representation space.

\textbf{Finding 4: Path-based reasoning enables mechanistic interpretability.} Unlike black-box graph neural network baselines or purely embedding-driven methods (e.g., GAT, HGTDR, DREAMwalk), BioPathNet++ surfaces explicit path-level explanations with attention weights. By leveraging 4-layer Neural Bellman--Ford Networks combined with multi-head attention across 7 predefined immune metapaths, the framework surfaces transparent, multi-hop biological routes linking candidate drugs to target diseases (e.g., Drug $\rightarrow$ inhibits $\rightarrow$ TNF-$\alpha$ $\rightarrow$ regulates $\rightarrow$ Disease, or Drug $\rightarrow$ modulates $\rightarrow$ JAK1/2 $\rightarrow$ activates $\rightarrow$ STAT3 $\rightarrow$ Disease). These mechanistic explanations render candidate predictions interpretable and directly prioritizable for translational follow-up.

\textbf{Finding 5: Clinical trial concordance independently validates learned representations.} Cross-referencing top predictions against registered clinical trials on ClinicalTrials.gov~\cite{clinicaltrials} yields a 100.0\% match rate across all six diseases, encompassing 1,575 unique clinical trial entries. This indicates that the architecture's top-ranked candidates---whether classified as ``known'' or ``novel'' relative to the training graph---consistently correspond to compounds that human investigators have independently deemed worthy of clinical investigation. This concordance provides strong orthogonal translational validation beyond computational pathway annotation.

\textbf{Finding 6: Cross-disease drug convergence reveals shared immune substrates.} The drug ranking analysis across all six diseases reveals that certain compounds (Budesonide in 4/6 diseases, Thalidomide in 3/6, Ciprofloxacin in 3/6) are independently predicted across multiple conditions. This convergence reflects the shared TNF/NF-$\kappa$B signaling substrate underlying autoimmune pathology and would not emerge from disease-specific models.

\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Pathway Database Cataloging Bias:} Five of six evaluated diseases exhibit an immune pathway match rate of 55.0\% (11/20), indicating that a substantial portion of predicted drugs act via indirect multi-hop gene interactions or through biological pathways not yet catalogued in canonical immune databases.
    \item \textit{Absence of Experimental Validation:} All evaluations are computational (PubMed text mining, pathway mapping, ClinicalTrials.gov cross-referencing); wet-lab in vitro or in vivo experimental assays have not been performed on novel candidates.
    \item \textit{Static Knowledge Graph Constraint:} The underlying knowledge graph is static and does not automatically ingest newly published biomedical literature or real-time clinical trial findings without manual re-ingestion.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper investigated the effectiveness of path-based graph neural architectures combined with cross-disease transfer learning for zero-shot drug candidate identification in rare autoimmune diseases. Through BioPathNet++---a framework combining DRKG enrichment with Orphanet/HPO annotations (84,712 nodes; 1,906,058 edges), 4-layer Neural Bellman--Ford multi-hop reasoning, 7 immune-specific metapaths, and joint pretraining on four data-rich autoimmune conditions---we conducted a systematic seven-layer evaluation across six zero-shot autoimmune targets.

Our findings demonstrate that: (1)~shared immune pathway representations learned from data-rich diseases transfer effectively to unseen conditions (test AUPRC 0.935, per-disease range 0.871--1.000), outperforming the TxGNN baseline~\cite{txgnn}; (2)~pretrained biomedical language model features are the dominant source of discriminative signal, outweighing graph-structural components by an order of magnitude, while graph reasoning contributes essential mechanistic interpretability; (3)~zero-shot transfer already captures near-optimal autoimmune mechanism coverage, with few-shot fine-tuning providing negligible additional benefit; (4)~drug ranking analysis across all six diseases reveals consistent recovery of known treatments alongside pharmacologically plausible novel candidates with cross-disease convergence; and (5)~100\% clinical trials concordance across 1,575 unique trials independently validates that the architecture's learned representations capture genuine biomedical relevance~\cite{clinicaltrials}.

The broader implication is that interpretable, mechanism-grounded computational drug repurposing for rare autoimmune diseases is technically feasible with current graph learning methods, provided appropriate data enrichment, path-constrained reasoning, and disease-stratified evaluation are applied.

\textbf{Future Directions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Dynamic Knowledge Graph Ingestion:} Developing automated NLP and text-mining pipelines to continuously update knowledge graph triples from newly published biomedical literature.
    \item \textit{Patient-Level Omics Integration:} Incorporating single-cell RNA sequencing and patient transcriptomics data to enable personalized, patient-stratified drug candidate prioritization~\cite{omics_repurposing}.
    \item \textit{Experimental Bio-Assay Validation:} Partnering with biomedical and translational research laboratories to conduct in vitro and in vivo validation of top-ranked novel candidates.
    \item \textit{Molecular Docking Integration:} Combining 3D protein--ligand structural docking simulations with graph path scores to refine candidate rankings based on physical binding affinity.
    \item \textit{Translational Clinical Validation:} Pursuing prospective observational studies and pilot trials for top-ranked repurposing candidates in collaboration with clinical rheumatologists and hepatologists.
\end{itemize}
