\title{Does Knowledge Transfer to Rare Autoimmune Diseases? Zero-Shot Drug Repurposing with Path-Based Graph Neural Networks}

\begin{abstract}
Rare autoimmune diseases such as primary biliary cholangitis, systemic sclerosis and myasthenia gravis have few approved drugs, so computational drug repurposing for them must work \emph{zero-shot}: it must rank drugs for a disease whose treatments were never seen in training. We ask three questions. (1) Does knowledge learned from data-rich autoimmune diseases transfer to rare ones? (2) Does a path-based graph neural network outperform simple repurposing rules in this setting? (3) Which components of the model matter? We use \sysname, a memory-efficient Neural Bellman--Ford network in the BioPathNet family, with an added transfer channel, on PrimeKG. We evaluate under a strict leakage-free protocol: each test disease is held out together with its disease--disease neighbours, and every drug is ranked against all 7,957 candidates. On 68 unseen diseases the model reaches an MRR of \MainValidMrr{} (\MainSeeds{} seeds, sd \MainValidMrrSd) versus \KnnValidMrr{} for the strongest baseline, a significant paired gain of \DiffMainKnnValid{} (Wilcoxon $p$\,\PwilMainKnnValid). A controlled transfer curve answers the first question. Trained only on rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and multiple sclerosis, the model ranks drugs for 12 held-out autoimmune diseases significantly better than when trained on label-matched non-autoimmune diseases (MRR \TrAEMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlEMrr; Wilcoxon $p$\,\PwilTrAActlE), largely by learning which drug classes treat autoimmune disease. With the full graph, however, removing all 123 autoimmune diseases leaves accuracy on the rare targets unchanged (\NoautoTargetsMrr{} vs.\ \MainTargetsMrr). Related autoimmune diseases are the most useful source of knowledge when data is scarce; at scale, the broad pharmacological graph supplies it. The model recovers the first-line drug for primary biliary cholangitis and four approved cholinesterase inhibitors for myasthenia gravis in its top four.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Drug repurposing finds new uses for approved drugs, whose safety profiles are already known, and is therefore faster and cheaper than de~novo development~\cite{pushpakom2019repurposing}. The need is greatest for rare diseases: as noted in~\cite{huang2024txgnn}, only 5--7\% of the more than 7,000 known rare diseases have an FDA-approved drug. Autoimmune disorders together affected 10.2\% of a 22-million-person UK cohort~\cite{conrad2023autoimmune}. Yet many of them, including primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), systemic sclerosis (SSc), Sj\"ogren syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), myasthenia gravis (MG) and Crohn disease, have few disease-specific drugs and little curated data.

Biomedical knowledge graphs (KGs) link drugs, proteins, phenotypes and diseases, and link prediction on them can rank candidate drugs~\cite{himmelstein2017hetionet,bonner2022review}. For a disease without known treatments the task is \emph{zero-shot}~\cite{huang2024txgnn}. Autoimmune diseases share immune pathways, so a natural idea is to learn from the data-rich ones, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and multiple sclerosis (MS), and transfer that knowledge to the rare ones. This paper tests that idea through three aims:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Transfer.} Does training on data-rich autoimmune diseases help rare ones, compared with matched non-autoimmune training data and with the full graph?
\item \textbf{Accuracy.} Does a path-based model beat simple, interpretable repurposing rules on diseases it has never seen?
\item \textbf{Components.} What do path reasoning, the transfer channel, the loss and the graph each contribute?
\end{enumerate}
We answer them with \sysname, a BioPathNet-style~\cite{hu2026biopathnet} Neural Bellman--Ford network (NBFNet)~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet}, evaluated under a strict leakage-free protocol, with seeds, confidence intervals and paired tests throughout.

\section{Related Work}
KG embeddings such as TransE, DistMult and RotatE~\cite{bordes2013transe,yang2015distmult,sun2019rotate} and relational GCNs~\cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn} have been used for repurposing in COVID-19~\cite{zhang2021covidkg,morselli2021covid}, rare diseases~\cite{sosa2020rare}, KG-Predict and DREAMwalk~\cite{gao2022kgpredict,bang2023dreamwalk}. They learn a vector per node from its training edges, so they degrade for diseases without known drugs~\cite{huang2024txgnn,bonner2022kge}. TxGNN targets zero-shot repurposing with a GNN and disease-similarity metric learning~\cite{huang2024txgnn}. NBFNet represents a (disease, drug) pair by message passing that starts at the disease, which makes it inductive and explainable through paths~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet}. BioPathNet adapts it to biomedical KGs and reports stronger zero-shot indication ranking than TxGNN~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}. \sysname follows BioPathNet's design; our contributions are the transfer study, the evaluation protocol and a full-graph implementation that runs on a single 16\,GB GPU. Leakage is a known pitfall: Bri\`ere et al.\ show that redundant triples and random or cold-start splits overestimate repurposing performance in biomedical KG benchmarks~\cite{briere2026leakage,kapoor2023leakage}.

\section{Data and Protocol}
\textbf{Knowledge graph.} We use PrimeKG~\cite{chandak2023primekg}: 129,375 nodes (7,957 drugs, 17,080 diseases), 4,007,433 non-therapeutic edges of 27 relation types (each given an explicit inverse) and 42,631 therapeutic drug--disease pairs. The model predicts \emph{indications}. For the six targets, labels are indications plus off-label uses (PBC 1, SSc 5, Sj\"ogren 12, AIH 1, MG 7, Crohn 24 drugs). Given so few labels, the targets serve as case studies, and statistical claims rest on larger held-out sets.

\textbf{Leakage-free zero-shot protocol.} A held-out disease is removed together with its disease--disease neighbours (its \emph{family}). Without this rule, all 16 Crohn indications remained reachable through ``Crohn's colitis''. No therapeutic edge of a held-out family ever enters the message graph, and each training batch also hides its own query diseases' drug edges, so training mimics zero-shot inference. Every drug is ranked; no negatives are sampled. An earlier version of our pipeline violated these rules: test pairs were graph edges, and a no-learning degree score reached AUROC 0.972. All results below come from the rebuilt pipeline. The six target families and 68 random validation diseases are held out; 1,206 diseases with indications remain for training.

\textbf{Transfer conditions (Aim~1).} The test set $E$ contains the 12 diseases with indications in TxGNN's autoimmune area~\cite{huang2024txgnn} that are neither targets nor in the RA/SLE/MS families. $E$ and the six targets are held out in every condition, which differ only in their training diseases:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A}: the RA/SLE/MS families only (11 diseases, 161 indications), the original transfer design;
\item \textbf{A$'$}: three random sets of 11 non-autoimmune diseases, each matched to A disease by disease on number of indications;
\item \textbf{D}: all \TrDNTrain{} non-autoimmune diseases (all 123 autoimmune nodes removed);
\item \textbf{C$_E$}: all \TrCENTrain{} diseases except $E$.
\end{itemize}
A versus A$'$ asks whether autoimmune data helps when data is scarce; C versus D asks whether it still helps at scale. PrimeKG has no further autoimmune diseases to form an ``all autoimmune'' condition: beyond RA/SLE/MS, its autoimmune diseases \emph{are} the test area.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Path channel.} For a query disease $u$, node states start from an indicator, $h^{(0)}_v=\mathbb{1}[v=u]\,q$, with a learned query vector $q$, and pass through $L=6$ layers
\begin{align}
m^{(t)}_v &= \frac{1}{\deg v}\sum_{(x,r,v)} h^{(t-1)}_x \odot w^{(t)}_r \;+\; \mathbb{1}[v=u]\,q,\\
h^{(t)}_v &= h^{(t-1)}_v + \mathrm{ReLU}\big(\mathrm{LN}\big(W^{(t)}[h^{(t-1)}_v;\, m^{(t)}_v;\, a_v m^{(t)}_v]\big)\big),
\end{align}
with DistMult relation vectors $w_r$ and a PNA-style degree scaler $a_v$~\cite{corso2020pna}. An MLP over $[h^{(1)}_d;\dots;h^{(L)}_d;q]$ scores every drug $d$ in one pass.

\textbf{Transfer channel.} A small MLP over four leakage-free features adds a second score: drug popularity among training diseases, a disease-similarity $k$NN score ($k=20$, TF--IDF over disease proteins and phenotypes), shared drug-target/disease-gene count, and frequency among autoimmune training diseases. Each is computed with the query's family excluded.

\textbf{Training.} A multi-positive softmax over all drugs is minimised. Message passing uses 60 relation-wise sparse matrices with a custom autograd function that recomputes products in the backward pass, so memory scales with nodes, not edges: 68,226 parameters, 8.5\,s per step of 8 diseases and 5.8\,GB peak on an NVIDIA T4. Adam (learning rate $5\times10^{-3}$) runs with early stopping on validation MRR. Hyperparameters were fixed in advance, not tuned.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Each known drug's rank among all drugs is \emph{filtered}~\cite{bordes2013transe}. We report MRR~\cite{voorhees1999trec} and Hits@$k$, the fraction of known drugs in the top $k$, averaged over diseases (random: MRR $\approx$0.001). AUROC is reported but not headlined: with thousands of negatives per positive, popularity alone reaches \PopValidAuroc~\cite{saito2015prc}. Six non-neural baselines run on every protocol; the strongest is disease $k$NN, which transfers the drugs of the 20 most similar training diseases. CIs are bootstraps over diseases~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. A difference counts as significant only if the paired bootstrap CI excludes zero \emph{and} a two-sided Wilcoxon test gives $p<0.05$~\cite{wilcoxon1945}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Aim 1: transfer to rare autoimmune diseases}
Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer} shows the transfer curve.
\emph{Scarce data.} Trained only on the RA/SLE/MS families (A), \sysname reaches MRR \TrAEMrr{} on $E$, against \TrActlEMrr{} when trained on label-matched non-autoimmune diseases (A$'$). The paired difference of \DiffTrAActlE{} (CI \DiffLoTrAActlE{} to \DiffHiTrAActlE; Wilcoxon $p$\,\PwilTrAActlE; better on \WinsTrAActlE{} of 12 diseases) is significant. It also holds when each run's final checkpoint is used instead of the early-stopped one (\DiffTrAActlELast, CI \DiffLoTrAActlELast{} to \DiffHiTrAActlELast, $p$\,\PwilTrAActlELast). The rare targets point the same way (\TrATargetsMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlTargetsMrr; $p$\,\PwilTrAActlTargets, $n=6$). The disease-$k$NN baseline shows the same pattern (\TrAEKnnMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlEKnnMrr), and in this regime the model is only slightly ahead of it. With 11 training diseases, the transferable knowledge is essentially a drug-class prior. The RA/SLE/MS families are treated mostly with corticosteroids and TNF inhibitors, and the A-trained model ranks the same corticosteroids first for every target. That is a reasonable first guess for an autoimmune disease, but it is not disease-specific reasoning.
\emph{Full scale.} Broad training data without any autoimmune disease (D) raises accuracy on $E$ to \TrDEMrr. Adding the autoimmune diseases (C$_E$, one seed) gives \TrCEEMrr{} (difference \DiffTrCEDE, $p$\,\PwilTrCEDE), with no measurable gain. On the rare targets, removing all autoimmune diseases likewise leaves MRR unchanged (\NoautoTargetsMrr{} vs.\ \MainTargetsMrr; \NoautoSeeds{} seeds each; $p$\,\PwilMainNoautoTargets).
Aim~1 is therefore answered in two parts. Knowledge from data-rich autoimmune diseases does transfer to rare ones, and it is the most useful knowledge when training data is scarce. At full scale, the broad pharmacological graph supplies the same information.

\subsection{Aim 2: accuracy on unseen diseases}
On the 68 validation diseases (Table~\ref{tab:main}), \sysname reaches MRR \MainValidMrr{} and H@10 \MainValidHitsTen{}, against \KnnValidMrr{} and \KnnValidHitsTen{} for disease $k$NN. The paired gain of \DiffMainKnnValid{} (95\% CI \DiffLoMainKnnValid{} to \DiffHiMainKnnValid) is significant ($p$\,\PwilMainKnnValid; better on \WinsMainKnnValid{} diseases, worse on \LossesMainKnnValid). For \MainValidAnyTen\% of unseen diseases an approved drug appears in the top 10 of 7,957 (disease $k$NN: \KnnValidAnyTen\%). AUROC is \MainValidAuroc. Seeds agree closely (sd \MainValidMrrSd). On a selection-free test split of 68 further diseases, never used for training or early stopping, the gain over disease $k$NN is \DiffTestKnn{} (MRR \TestMrr{} vs.\ \KnnTestMrr; $p$\,\PwilTestKnn; \TestSeeds{} seed so far). On the six targets the gain (\DiffMainKnnTargets) points the same way for \WinsMainKnnTargets{} of 6 diseases but is not significant with $n=6$ ($p$\,\PwilMainKnnTargets).

\subsection{Aim 3: what each component contributes}
Path reasoning carries the signal. Paths alone reach \PathsValidMrr{}, tied with the full model ($p$\,\PwilMainPathsValid), while the transfer channel alone reaches only \PriorValidMrr. The transfer channel does not raise mean accuracy, but it reduces seed variance on the targets (sd \MainTargetsMrrSd{} vs.\ \PathsTargetsMrrSd). In single-seed ablations, the listwise loss beats NBFNet's self-adversarial BCE (\MainValidMrr{} vs.\ \BceValidMrr; $p$\,\PwilMainBceValid). A BioPathNet-style background graph without tissue-expression and drug--drug edges removes \BrgEdgeCutPct\% of edges at unchanged accuracy (\BrgValidMrr; $p$\,\PwilMainBrgValid) and shortens training steps by \BrgSpeedupPct\%.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Our MRR values fall within the range BioPathNet reports for TxGNN's zero-shot disease areas (about 0.2--0.8)~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}, although the protocols differ and we make no head-to-head claim. Limitations: (i) the targets have 1--24 labelled drugs, so per-target metrics are noisy and can be lower bounds (AIH); (ii) the validation diseases also select the stopping epoch, which adds \SelGapMainMin--\SelGapMainMax{} MRR of optimism, far below the gain over disease $k$NN and confirmed by the selection-free test split; (iii) the transfer test set $E$ has only 12 diseases, and models trained on 11 diseases are unstable across seeds; (iv) TxGNN's autoimmune area also contains autoinflammatory diseases; (v) predictions are hypotheses for experts, not treatment advice.

\section{Conclusion}
Knowledge does transfer to rare autoimmune diseases that the model never sees. When training data is scarce, related autoimmune diseases are its most useful source. A path-based NBFNet model significantly outperforms strong similarity baselines for unseen diseases and recovers established therapies for several rare autoimmune diseases. At full scale, the broad pharmacological graph supplies the same knowledge, and autoimmune training data adds nothing measurable. Path reasoning drives the accuracy, and the transfer channel contributes stability. Code, splits and run logs are released.
