\title{BioPathNet++ for Autoimmune Diseases: Path-Based Transfer Learning for Rare Disease Drug Repurposing}

\author{
\IEEEauthorblockN{Venkata Naga Sai Yatin Tatikonda, Srijan Jha, Charan Kumar Reddy Arikeri, Praveen Kumar Yadav, Priyanka H}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Computer Science and Engineering, PES University, Bengaluru, India}
}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Rare autoimmune diseases have few approved drugs, so computational repurposing must rank drugs for diseases whose treatments were never seen in training (\emph{zero-shot}). We study this setting with \sysname, a BioPathNet-style path-based graph neural network with an added channel of four leakage-free priors, on the PrimeKG knowledge graph. Our protocol holds out each test disease together with its related diseases and ranks all 7,957 drugs. On 68 unseen test diseases, a known indication is in the top 10 for \TabModelTestAnyTen\% of diseases (random: \RandExpAnyTenTest\%), and the mean reciprocal rank exceeds that of the best baseline (\TestMrr{} vs.\ \KnnTestMrr; $p$\,\PwilTestKnn). With scarce training data, autoimmune source diseases transfer better than label-matched non-autoimmune ones (\TrAEMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlEMrr; $p$\,\PwilTrAActlE); with the full graph they do not help and even lower accuracy. Path reasoning carries the accuracy; the prior channel mainly adds stability. The output is a ranked list of hypotheses for expert review, not treatment advice.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
drug repurposing, transfer learning, zero-shot learning, knowledge graphs, graph neural networks, autoimmune diseases
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Drug repurposing finds new uses for approved drugs, whose safety is already known, and is faster and cheaper than de~novo development~\cite{pushpakom2019repurposing}. The need is greatest for rare diseases: only 5--7\% of the more than 7,000 rare diseases have an FDA-approved drug~\cite{huang2024txgnn}. Autoimmune disorders affected 10.2\% of a 22-million-person UK cohort~\cite{conrad2023autoimmune}, yet primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), systemic sclerosis (SSc), Sj\"ogren syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), myasthenia gravis (MG) and Crohn disease have few disease-specific drugs and little curated data.

Knowledge graphs (KGs) link drugs, proteins, phenotypes and diseases, and link prediction on them ranks candidate drugs~\cite{himmelstein2017hetionet,bonner2022review}. For a disease without known treatments the task is \emph{zero-shot}~\cite{huang2024txgnn}: knowledge learned from diseases with known drugs must \emph{transfer} to diseases whose drugs the model has never seen. Because autoimmune diseases share immune pathways, the best source of that knowledge might be the data-rich ones: rheumatoid arthritis (RA), lupus (SLE) and multiple sclerosis (MS). We ask three questions. \textbf{Aim~1, source of transfer:} must the knowledge come from related autoimmune diseases, or does broad pharmacology suffice? \textbf{Aim~2, accuracy:} does a path-based model beat simple repurposing rules on unseen diseases? \textbf{Aim~3, components:} what do path reasoning, the prior channel, the loss and the graph each contribute?

\textbf{What is new relative to BioPathNet.} \sysname uses the design of BioPathNet~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}, a Neural Bellman--Ford network (NBFNet)~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}); we do not claim a new architecture. Our contributions are (i)~a \emph{strict-family} zero-shot protocol that also hides each test disease's related diseases, motivated by a leakage audit of our own earlier pipeline; (ii)~a \emph{controlled transfer study} that compares autoimmune source diseases with label-matched non-autoimmune controls; (iii)~a full-graph implementation whose memory is linear in the number of nodes, plus a prior channel that stabilizes training; (iv)~a head-to-head run of TxGNN's own code, showing that the winner depends on the candidate set; and (v)~external checks of new candidates against trials, PubMed and later approvals.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Biomedical KGs.} Hetionet combined 29 public resources and ranked candidates with metapath features~\cite{himmelstein2017hetionet}. DRKG~\cite{drkg2020} and PrimeKG~\cite{chandak2023primekg} followed; PrimeKG separates indications, contraindications and off-label uses for 17,080 diseases. \textbf{Embeddings and GNNs.} KG embeddings~\cite{bordes2013transe,yang2015distmult,sun2019rotate} and relational GCNs~\cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn} have been used for COVID-19~\cite{zhang2021covidkg,morselli2021covid}, rare diseases~\cite{sosa2020rare} and general repurposing~\cite{gao2022kgpredict,bang2023dreamwalk}. Their reported gains depend strongly on how the dataset is built and evaluated~\cite{bonner2022kge,ali2021pykeen}, and because they learn one vector per node from its training edges, they degrade for diseases without known drugs~\cite{huang2024txgnn}.

\textbf{Zero-shot and path-based models.} TxGNN handles such diseases with disease-similarity metric learning and reports 49.2\% better zero-shot indication prediction than eight other methods~\cite{huang2024txgnn}. NBFNet scores a (disease, drug) pair by Bellman--Ford message passing that starts at the disease, which makes it inductive and explainable by paths~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet}; BioPathNet adapts it to biomedical KGs and evaluates it on TxGNN's disease-area splits~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}. Recent rare-disease studies instead build disease-specific graphs and explainable GNNs~\cite{papadimas2026neuromuscular,perdomo2026rdexplainer}.

\textbf{Leakage.} Redundant triples and loose splits overestimate repurposing accuracy~\cite{briere2026leakage,kapoor2023leakage}, and under heavy class imbalance AUROC can look high for weak rankings~\cite{saito2015prc}. Table~\ref{tab:related} positions \sysname: it reuses BioPathNet's model and adds a stricter hold-out unit and a controlled transfer study.

\section{Data and Protocol}
\textbf{Knowledge graph and labels.} We use PrimeKG~\cite{chandak2023primekg}: 129,375 nodes (7,957 drugs, 17,080 diseases), 4,007,433 edges of 27 non-therapeutic relation types and 42,631 drug--disease pairs of three types: \emph{indication} (the drug is labeled for the disease), \emph{off-label use} and \emph{contraindication}. We call indications and off-label uses \emph{known drugs}, and use ``approved'' only for regulatory approvals that we checked ourselves. Validation and test positives are indications only. The rare targets have so few that their labels are indications plus off-label uses: PBC 1+0, SSc 3+2, Sj\"ogren 2+10, AIH 0+1, MG 4+3, Crohn 16+8. Contraindications are never positives. With so few labels, the targets are case studies; statistical claims rest on larger held-out sets.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} PrimeKG's file lists every edge in both directions (8,100,498 rows). We keep one canonical direction per relation, drop duplicates, and add an explicit inverse for every relation (60 relation types in all). Drug--disease pairs are kept in a separate table, and each split's message graph receives only those of its training diseases. The path channel uses no node features or text embeddings: nodes differ only by their edges (\texttt{bpp/build\_primekg.py}).

\textbf{Leakage-free zero-shot protocol.} A held-out disease is removed with its \emph{family}: its disease--disease neighbors and, for targets, the subtypes that TxGNN's name-based grouping assigns to it. Without this rule, all 16 Crohn indications stayed reachable through ``Crohn's colitis''. No drug edge of a held-out family enters the message graph, and each training batch also hides its own diseases' drug edges, so training mimics zero-shot inference. An earlier version of our pipeline broke these rules: test pairs were graph edges, and a degree score with no learning reached AUROC 0.972. The six target families and 68 random validation diseases are held out; 1,206 diseases with indications remain for training.

\textbf{Transfer conditions (Aim~1).} The test set $E$ holds the 12 diseases with indications in TxGNN's autoimmune area (descendants of DOID:417~\cite{huang2024txgnn}) that are neither targets nor RA/SLE/MS relatives. $E$ and the targets are held out in every condition: \textbf{A} trains on the RA/SLE/MS families only (11 diseases, 161 indications); \textbf{A$'$} on three random sets of 11 non-autoimmune diseases, each matched to A disease by disease on number of indications; \textbf{D} on all \TrDNTrain{} non-autoimmune diseases; \textbf{C$_E$} on all \TrCENTrain{} diseases except $E$. A vs.\ A$'$ asks whether autoimmune data helps when data is scarce, C$_E$ vs.\ D whether it still helps at scale.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Path channel.} For a query disease $u$, node states start from an indicator, $h^{(0)}_v=\mathbf{1}[v=u]\,q$, with a learned query vector $q$, and pass through $L=6$ layers
\begin{align}
m^{(t)}_v &= \frac{1}{\deg v}\sum_{(x,r,v)} h^{(t-1)}_x \odot w^{(t)}_r \;+\; \mathbf{1}[v=u]\,q,\label{eq:msg}\\
h^{(t)}_v &= h^{(t-1)}_v + \mathrm{ReLU}\big(\mathrm{LN}\big(W^{(t)}[h^{(t-1)}_v;\, m^{(t)}_v;\, a_v m^{(t)}_v]\big)\big),\label{eq:upd}
\end{align}
with DistMult relation vectors $w_r$ in (\ref{eq:msg}) and a PNA-style degree scaler $a_v$~\cite{corso2020pna} in (\ref{eq:upd}). An MLP over $[h^{(1)}_d;\dots;h^{(L)}_d;q]$ scores every drug $d$ in one pass (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The messages use one sparse matrix per relation and a custom autograd function that recomputes products in the backward pass, so memory grows with nodes, not edges.

\textbf{Prior channel and training.} A small MLP over four features adds a second score: drug popularity among training diseases, a disease-similarity $k$NN score ($k=20$, TF--IDF over disease proteins and phenotypes), shared drug-target/disease-gene count, and frequency among autoimmune training diseases, each computed with the query's family excluded. The loss is the mean negative log-softmax probability of each disease's known drugs over all drugs. Hyperparameters were fixed in advance, not tuned.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Every drug is ranked, and ranks are \emph{filtered}: a disease's other known drugs are removed before ranking each one~\cite{bordes2013transe}. Table~\ref{tab:metricdefs} defines the metrics. MRR~\cite{voorhees1999trec} rewards known drugs at the very top: on the test split, MRR \TestMrr{} corresponds to a median known-drug rank of \MedRankTest, with \RankOneShareTest\% ranked first. AUROC is high even for popularity (\PopValidAuroc), so it is never used alone~\cite{saito2015prc}. The 68 validation diseases select the stopping epoch; 68 further \emph{test} diseases are used neither for training nor for stopping. Of six non-neural baselines and three KG embeddings (TransE, DistMult, RotatE, trained on the same graph), the strongest is disease $k$NN, which transfers the drugs of the 20 most similar training diseases. CIs are bootstraps over diseases~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}; a difference is significant only if the paired CI excludes zero \emph{and} a two-sided Wilcoxon test gives $p<0.05$~\cite{wilcoxon1945}.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} Data: PrimeKG (Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/IXA7BM) and TxGNN's area files and code (GitHub, commit 0e0203c). Splits: validation seed 0, test seed 1, matched controls seeds 100--102, all stored as JSON. Model: dimension 32, 6 layers, readout MLP $224\!\to\!64\!\to\!1$ (dropout 0.1), prior MLP $4\!\to\!16\!\to\!1$; 68,226 parameters. Training: Adam, learning rate $5\times10^{-3}$, 8 diseases per batch, gradient clipping 1.0, $\leq$20 epochs, patience 5--6 on validation MRR (transfer runs: $\leq$60 epochs, patience 10); seeds 0--2; one NVIDIA T4 (16\,GB) per run, 8.5\,s per step, 5.8\,GB peak. Statistics: 1,000 bootstrap resamples (5,000 for paired tests). Code, splits, logs and the script that generates every number: \url{https://github.com/SaiYatin/Capstone_Drug_Repurposing_Project} (branch \texttt{bpp-v3}).

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Aim 1: transfer to rare autoimmune diseases}
\emph{Scarce data} (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}). Trained only on the RA/SLE/MS families (A), \sysname reaches MRR \TrAEMrr{} on $E$, against \TrActlEMrr{} with label-matched non-autoimmune diseases (A$'$): a significant difference of \DiffTrAActlE{} (CI \DiffLoTrAActlE{} to \DiffHiTrAActlE; $p$\,\PwilTrAActlE; better on \WinsTrAActlE{} of 12), which also holds for final instead of early-stopped checkpoints ($p$\,\PwilTrAActlELast). The rare targets point the same way (\TrATargetsMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlTargetsMrr; $p$\,\PwilTrAActlTargets, $n=6$), and so does disease $k$NN (\TrAEKnnMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlEKnnMrr). What transfers is a drug-class prior: the A-trained model ranks the same corticosteroids first for every target, a sensible first guess but not disease-specific reasoning. With paths only, A and A$'$ both stay near the floor (\TrAPathsEMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlPathsEMrr), so the advantage comes from the prior channel.

\emph{Full scale.} Training on all non-autoimmune diseases (D) reaches \TrDEMrr{} on $E$; adding the autoimmune diseases (C$_E$, \TrCESeeds{} seeds) lowers it to \TrCEEMrr{} ($p$\,\PwilTrCEDE; same validation set), and disease $k$NN drops too (\TrDEKnnMrr{} to \TrCEEKnnMrr). On the targets, removing all autoimmune diseases leaves MRR unchanged (\NoautoTargetsMrr{} vs.\ \MainTargetsMrr; $p$\,\PwilMainNoautoTargets). The best source thus depends on data size: related autoimmune diseases when data is scarce, the broad pharmacological graph at full scale.

\subsection{Aim 2: accuracy on unseen diseases}
On the selection-free test split (Table~\ref{tab:main}), a known indication is in the top 10 for \TabModelTestAnyTen\% of diseases, against \TabKnnTestAnyTen\% for disease $k$NN, \TabPopTestAnyTen\% for popularity and \RandExpAnyTenTest\% by chance. MRR is \TestMrr{} vs.\ \KnnTestMrr{} (difference \DiffTestKnn, CI \DiffLoTestKnn{} to \DiffHiTestKnn; $p$\,\PwilTestKnn). On the 68 validation diseases the gain over $k$NN is \DiffMainKnnValid{} ($p$\,\PwilMainKnnValid). \sysname therefore outperforms every baseline evaluated on the same leakage-free split. On the six targets it is ahead for \WinsMainKnnTargets{} of 6, not significant with $n=6$.

\subsection{Aim 3: what each component contributes}
Path reasoning carries the signal. On the 68 validation diseases, paths alone reach MRR \PathsValidMrr{}, tied with the full model's \MainValidMrr{} ($p$\,\PwilMainPathsValid); the prior channel alone reaches \PriorValidMrr. These validation values are not comparable with the test-split MRR of \TestMrr{}: that is the full model on 68 \emph{other} diseases, which are easier for every method ($k$NN \KnnTestMrr{} on test vs.\ \KnnValidMrr{} on validation). The prior channel does not raise the mean but reduces seed variance on the targets (sd \MainTargetsMrrSd{} vs.\ \PathsTargetsMrrSd). Self-adversarial BCE is not significantly worse than the listwise loss (\BceValidMrr{} vs.\ \MainValidMrr; $p$\,\PwilMainBceValid), and dropping tissue-expression and drug--drug edges removes \BrgEdgeCutPct\% of edges at unchanged accuracy ($p$\,\PwilMainBrgValid).

\subsection{Case studies and validation}
\emph{Known drugs.} Ursodeoxycholic acid ranks first for PBC in every seed, and the four labeled cholinesterase inhibitors hold ranks 1--4 for MG. AIH scores near zero only because PrimeKG lists one off-label drug for it, while the model's top choice, prednisolone, is standard AIH therapy (comparator in NCT00838214).
\emph{Unlabeled candidates} (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). We searched ClinicalTrials.gov for each candidate and for two reference sets (popularity top 20; 20 random drugs per target). The drug must be a named intervention, matched by whole name (A: phase $\geq$3, B: phase 2, C: phase 1/observational). A trial \emph{tests} the drug only if its title names it or the drug is in some but not all arms, which excludes background therapy, premedication and transplant conditioning. Of the model's candidates, \ValTrialTestedModel\% have such a trial (\ValGradeATested{} at phase $\geq$3), against \ValTrialTestedPop\% for popularity and \ValTrialTestedRand\% for random drugs; examples are amifampridine for MG (NCT03304054), ciprofloxacin for Crohn (NCT00609973) and N-acetylcysteine for SSc (NCT00428883), all checked by hand. A trial is not proof of benefit: amifampridine helped in MuSK-antibody MG~\cite{bonanno2018amifampridine} but not in acetylcholine-receptor-antibody MG~\cite{remijn2026amifampridine}. Ustekinumab for Crohn counts as unlabeled only because PrimeKG misses its 2016 approval. PubMed titles were classified with AI assistance and every benefit or harm label was re-checked against the abstract. PubMed shows benefit for \ValLitSupModel\% of the model's candidates (popularity \ValLitSupPop\%, random \ValLitSupRand\%) and harm or no benefit for \ValLitAgainstModel\% (popularity \ValLitAgainstPop\%): three corticosteroid pairs and N-acetylcysteine for SSc, whereas popularity's are mostly antibiotics that can worsen MG or induce hepatitis.

\emph{Temporal check.} Six drugs approved after the PrimeKG snapshot (risankizumab, upadacitinib, guselkumab for Crohn; ravulizumab, rozanolixizumab for MG; elafibranor for PBC) rank at a median of \TempPostMedRank{} of 7,957; \TempPostBetter{} of 6 beat chance, not significant ($p$\,\TempPostP).
\emph{Explanations and bias.} Gradient-based path extraction~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet} shows, for example, that amifampridine reaches MG through its indication for Lambert--Eaton syndrome, a related neuromuscular-junction disease, and exposes weak links such as ciprofloxacin reaching Crohn through ``mouth disease''. Corticosteroids fill \ValSteroidShare\% and antibacterials \ValAntibioticShare\% of top-20 slots, so the lists need expert filtering.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Our MRR values lie within the range BioPathNet reports on TxGNN's disease areas (about 0.2--0.8)~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}, but splits differ, so we ran TxGNN's own code on its autoimmune split, with our model trained only on edges that TxGNN also trains on (\TxhSeedsTx{} seeds, \TxhN{} diseases). Ranking all drugs, \sysname is significantly better (MRR \TxhOursMrr{} vs.\ \TxhTxMrr, $p$\,\TxhPwilFullMrr): only \TxhTxIndShare\% of TxGNN's top-100 drugs have any indication, against \TxhOursIndShare\% for ours. Ranking only drugs with indications, as TxGNN does, no difference is significant (Recall@100 \TxhOursRecall{} vs.\ \TxhTxRecall; AUROC \TxhOursAuroc{} vs.\ \TxhTxAuroc). BioPathNet was not re-run.
Limitations: (i) the targets have 1--24 labeled drugs, so their metrics are noisy and can be lower bounds (AIH); (ii) the validation diseases also choose the stopping epoch (\SelGapMainMin--\SelGapMainMax{} MRR of optimism), which the test split avoids; (iii) $E$ has only 12 diseases, and 11-disease models are unstable across seeds; (iv) literature labels have not been reviewed by a clinician; (v) corticosteroids and antibiotics dominate the lists.

\section{Conclusion}
Path-based zero-shot transfer works for rare autoimmune diseases the model never sees. When training data is scarce, related autoimmune diseases are the most useful source; at full scale the broad pharmacological graph is the better source. \sysname outperforms every baseline evaluated on the same leakage-free split, recovers established therapies, and ranks later-approved drugs high but not significantly above chance. Path reasoning drives accuracy, and the prior channel adds stability.
