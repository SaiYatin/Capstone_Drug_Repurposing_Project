\title{Leakage-Controlled Zero- and Few-Shot Drug Repurposing for Rare Autoimmune Diseases: A Transfer Study with a Path-Based Graph Neural Network}

\author{TVNS~Yatin, Srijan~Jha, A~Charan~KR, Praveen~Kumar~Yadav, and~Priyanka~H%
\thanks{The authors are with the Department of Computer Science and Engineering, PES University, Bengaluru, India.}}

\markboth{Manuscript draft}{Yatin \MakeLowercase{\textit{et al.}}: Leakage-Controlled Drug Repurposing for Rare Autoimmune Diseases}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Rare autoimmune diseases have few approved drugs, so computational repurposing must rank drugs for diseases whose treatments are unknown. We study this setting on the PrimeKG knowledge graph under a leakage-controlled protocol that holds out each test disease together with its related diseases and ranks all 7,957 drugs. Our model, \sysname, reuses the path-based BioPathNet architecture and adds a channel of four leakage-free priors; we do not propose a new layer. The contributions are the protocol, a controlled transfer study, a few-shot evaluation, a head-to-head with TxGNN and external validation. On 68 unseen test diseases a known indication is in the top 10 for \TabModelTestAnyTen\% of diseases (random: \RandExpAnyTenTest\%), and the mean reciprocal rank (MRR) of \TestMrr{} exceeds every baseline, including knowledge-graph embeddings (best baseline \KnnTestMrr). When training data is scarce, autoimmune source diseases transfer better than label-matched controls (\TrAEMrr{} vs.\ \TrActlEMrr); at full scale they even lower MRR (\TrCEEMrr{} vs.\ \TrDEMrr). Revealing three known drugs raises MRR from \FsBppZeroMrr{} to \FsBppThreeMrr{} without retraining, and fusing the model with co-indication filtering gives \FsRrfThreeMrr{} (best baseline \FsKnnCoindThreeMrr). On TxGNN's own split \sysname ranks all drugs significantly better. Its outputs are hypotheses for expert review.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{D}{rug} \textbf{repurposing} identifies new indications for approved drugs. Because these drugs already have known safety profiles, development is shorter and cheaper than for new molecules~\cite{pushpakom2019repurposing}. Rare diseases need repurposing most: of more than 7,000 rare diseases only 5--7\% have an FDA-approved drug~\cite{huang2024txgnn}, and together they affect 3.5--5.9\% of the population~\cite{nguengang2020rare}. Autoimmune disorders are a related unmet need: 19 common autoimmune diseases affected 10.2\% of a 22-million-person UK cohort~\cite{conrad2023autoimmune}, and many individual autoimmune conditions are rare and have few disease-specific drugs. We study six of them: primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), systemic sclerosis (SSc), Sj\"ogren syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), myasthenia gravis (MG) and Crohn disease.

\textbf{Biomedical knowledge graphs} (KGs) integrate drugs, proteins, phenotypes, pathways and diseases, and link prediction on them proposes drug--disease pairs~\cite{himmelstein2017hetionet,bonner2022review,wei2025kgreview,kumar2025scoping}. Most work evaluates on randomly held-out \emph{edges}, which rewards copying the drugs of near-identical diseases. The clinical question is different: what could treat a disease for which nothing, or almost nothing, is known to work? TxGNN~\cite{huang2024txgnn} formalised this \textbf{zero-shot} setting by holding out whole diseases, and BioPathNet~\cite{hu2026biopathnet} showed that path-based reasoning with Neural Bellman--Ford networks (NBFNet)~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet} ranks zero-shot indications well on PrimeKG~\cite{chandak2023primekg}.

We use \textbf{transfer} in this zero-shot sense: knowledge learned from diseases with known treatments is applied, without retraining, to diseases whose treatments the model has never seen. Autoimmune diseases share immune pathways, so the most useful source of that knowledge for rare autoimmune diseases might be the data-rich ones: rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and multiple sclerosis (MS). We ask three questions. (1)~\emph{Source of transfer:} must the knowledge come from related autoimmune diseases, or does broad pharmacology suffice (Section~\ref{sec:aim1})? (2)~\emph{Accuracy:} does a path-based model beat simple repurposing rules and KG embeddings on unseen diseases (Section~\ref{sec:aim2})? (3)~\emph{Components:} what do path reasoning, the prior channel, the loss and the graph contribute (Section~\ref{sec:aim3})? Because a few drugs are often known even for a rare disease, we also ask how much a handful of known drugs helps (\textbf{few-shot}, Section~\ref{sec:fewshot}).

\subsection{What is new relative to BioPathNet}
\label{sec:novelty}
\sysname is \emph{not} a new architecture. Its path channel is BioPathNet's NBFNet design~\cite{hu2026biopathnet,zhu2021nbfnet}, and the name only marks the additions in Table~\ref{tab:novelty}. The novelty of this work lies in the questions it answers and in how it measures the answers:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{strict-family zero-shot protocol} that also hides each test disease's related diseases, motivated by a \textbf{leakage audit} of our own earlier pipeline, in which a degree heuristic matched a trained GNN (Section~\ref{sec:audit});
\item a \textbf{controlled transfer study} that compares autoimmune source diseases with label-matched non-autoimmune controls, from 11 training diseases to the full graph;
\item a \textbf{few-shot evaluation} that reveals one to three known drugs of an unseen disease, with collaborative-filtering baselines;
\item a \textbf{prior channel} of four leakage-free features and a \textbf{memory-efficient full-graph implementation} that trains on one 16\,GB GPU;
\item a \textbf{head-to-head with TxGNN} on TxGNN's own split, which shows that the winner depends on the set of drugs being ranked;
\item stronger \textbf{baselines} (KG embeddings, disease $k$NN, collaborative filtering), repeated seeds, and \textbf{external validation} of new candidates against trials, PubMed and later drug approvals.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Knowledge graphs for repurposing.} Hetionet integrated 29 public resources and used metapath features to prioritise candidates~\cite{himmelstein2017hetionet}. DRKG~\cite{drkg2020} and PrimeKG~\cite{chandak2023primekg} followed; PrimeKG covers 17,080 diseases with indication, contraindication and off-label edges. Recent reviews trace the field from classical machine learning to graph neural networks~\cite{wei2025kgreview} and catalogue multi-relation learning methods~\cite{kumar2025scoping}; Bonner et al.\ show that KG-embedding results depend strongly on dataset construction and evaluation~\cite{bonner2022review,bonner2022kge}.

\textbf{Embedding and GNN methods.} Translational and bilinear embeddings (TransE, DistMult, RotatE)~\cite{bordes2013transe,yang2015distmult,sun2019rotate} and relational GCNs~\cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn} are standard link predictors. They were applied to COVID-19~\cite{zhang2021covidkg,morselli2021covid}, polypharmacy side effects~\cite{zitnik2018decagon}, KG-Predict~\cite{gao2022kgpredict} and DREAMwalk, which reports up to 16.8\% higher drug--disease association accuracy than link-prediction models~\cite{bang2023dreamwalk}. A unified benchmark showed that many reported embedding gains shrink under consistent training and evaluation~\cite{ali2021pykeen}. Embeddings learn one vector per node from its training edges, so a disease without known drugs gets little supervision. KGiA augments KGs with disease-specific counterfactual edges to reach previously unseen entities~\cite{oguztuzun2025kgia}.

\textbf{Zero-shot and path-based methods.} TxGNN combines a relational GNN with disease-similarity metric learning and, against eight methods, improves indication prediction by 49.2\% under zero-shot evaluation; its explainer shows multi-hop paths~\cite{huang2024txgnn}. NBFNet represents a (source, target) pair by generalised Bellman--Ford message passing from the source, which makes it inductive and explainable by paths~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet}, in the spirit of subgraph reasoning~\cite{teru2020grail}; A*Net and ULTRA scale and generalise the idea~\cite{zhu2023astarnet,galkin2024ultra}. BioPathNet adapts NBFNet to biomedical KGs with a background regulatory graph and stringent negative sampling; on TxGNN's zero-shot splits it reports indication MRR from about 0.2 (cardiovascular) to above 0.6 (cell proliferation, adrenal gland)~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}.

\textbf{Rare diseases and few-shot repurposing.} Rare-disease studies build disease-specific KGs: literature graphs for rare neuro-muscular disorders with an ensemble of rule-based, GNN and path models~\cite{papadimas2026neuromuscular}, explainable GNNs for single rare diseases~\cite{perdomo2026rdexplainer}, semantically valid negative samples for Huntington's disease~\cite{bianchi2026negatives}, and literature-based embeddings~\cite{sosa2020rare}. Yang et al.\ cast repurposing as few-shot link prediction with meta-learning~\cite{yang2024fewshot}. We instead evaluate a zero-shot-trained model when a few known drugs are revealed, against collaborative filtering~\cite{sarwar2001itemcf}. Table~\ref{tab:related} positions this work.

\textbf{Leakage.} Leakage is a main cause of irreproducible ML-based science~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}. In KG link prediction it appears as test edges left in the graph, inverse or duplicate relations and near-duplicate entities. Random negatives add a further problem: node popularity alone separates the classes, so AUROC is uninformative when positives are rare~\cite{saito2015prc,davis2006prroc}. Bri\`ere et al.\ showed for biomedical KG embeddings that removing train--test redundancy lowers performance and that random and cold-start splits overestimate repurposing performance on Orphanet rare-disease indications; their permutation tests did not find that degree alone drives those models~\cite{briere2026leakage}. Our audit concerns a different failure, random negatives in evaluation, under which a degree score alone suffices.

\section{Dataset and Code}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{PrimeKG.} We use PrimeKG~\cite{chandak2023primekg}, a precision-medicine KG built from 20 resources, downloaded from Harvard Dataverse (doi:10.7910/DVN/IXA7BM; file \texttt{kg.csv}). Table~\ref{tab:data} gives its size. Its 27 non-therapeutic relation types (protein--protein, drug--protein, disease--protein, disease--phenotype, anatomy--protein expression, drug--drug, pathways, exposures and others) form the \emph{context} graph. PrimeKG has three drug--disease relations: \emph{indication} (the drug is labelled for the disease), \emph{off-label use} and \emph{contraindication}. We call indications and off-label uses \emph{known drugs} and use ``approved'' only for regulatory approvals we checked ourselves.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} PrimeKG's edge file lists every edge in both directions. For each relation we fix one canonical direction (by node type, or by node index within one type), drop duplicates and only then add an explicit inverse, so no edge enters the graph twice; with the three drug--disease relations this gives 60 relation types. Drug--disease pairs are stored in a separate table and never become context edges; for each split the message graph receives only the pairs of training diseases. The path channel uses no node features or text embeddings; nodes differ only by their edges. The build takes about 35\,s on a laptop (\texttt{bpp/build\_primekg.py}) and produces a 9\,MB dataset used in every run.

\textbf{Labels.} The model predicts indications, and validation and test positives are indications only. The rare targets have so few that their labels are indications plus off-label uses: PBC 1+0, SSc 3+2, Sj\"ogren 2+10, AIH 0+1, MG 4+3 and Crohn 16+8. Contraindications are never positives. The targets are therefore case studies; quantitative claims rest on the 68 validation, 68 test and 21 area diseases.

\textbf{Code and reproducibility.} All code, splits, the audit script, run logs and the analysis script that generates every number, table and figure of this paper are at \url{https://github.com/SaiYatin/Capstone_Drug_Repurposing_Project} (branch \texttt{bpp-v3}). The paper's numbers are \LaTeX{} macros written by \texttt{bpp/analyze.py} from the run logs; none is typed by hand.

\section{Why a Strict Protocol: Audit of a Naive Pipeline}
\label{sec:audit}
Our earlier pipeline built a graph from an enriched DRKG extract (84,712 nodes), trained an NBFNet-style model and reported test AUPRC between 0.935 and 0.999. A stand-alone audit script, released with the code, found three mechanisms behind these scores. (i)~\emph{Target leakage}: the message graph was built from the full KG, so all 277 test pairs were edges the model could traverse. (ii)~\emph{Trivial negatives}: negatives were random drug--disease pairs, and a zero-parameter score $\log\deg(\text{drug})+\log\deg(\text{disease})$ reached test AUROC 0.972, above the model's 0.941. (iii)~\emph{One-directional subgraphs}: without inverse relations, five of six targets were unreachable and scored by drug features alone. In addition, 90--91\% of positive labels were text-mined edges (one of them, thalidomide for PBC, contradicts a negative randomised trial~\cite{mccormick1994thalidomide}), a phenotype parser created 2,325 spurious relation types, language-model features were computed from identifier strings, and ablation scripts loaded the wrong model. Every number below comes from the rebuilt pipeline.

\section{Zero- and Few-Shot Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Strict-family hold-out}
Holding out a disease node alone is not enough: subtypes and synonyms linked by disease--disease edges often carry the same drugs. Without further removal all 16 Crohn indications stayed reachable through ``Crohn's colitis'', MG through ``adult-onset myasthenia gravis'' and SSc through ``scleroderma''. A disease's \emph{family} is therefore the disease plus its disease--disease neighbours; for the targets and RA/SLE/MS it also contains the disease nodes that TxGNN's name-based grouping assigns to the same group, which catches numbered subtypes. We hold out whole families:
\begin{itemize}
\item no drug--disease edge of any held-out family member is ever in the message graph;
\item validation diseases (5\% of labelled diseases, seed 0) are held out with their families, so model selection cannot reward sibling copying;
\item in every training step the drug edges of the batch's query diseases and their families are also removed, so training matches zero-shot inference.
\end{itemize}
The rules live in the graph builder, and unit tests check that removed edges are absent from the propagation matrices and that no training and evaluation families overlap.

\subsection{Selection-free test split}
\label{sec:testsplit}
Validation diseases select the stopping epoch, so their scores are slightly optimistic. We therefore draw 68 further non-autoimmune labelled diseases (seed 1) as a test set, held out with their families (1,065 training diseases remain) and used neither for training nor for early stopping.

\subsection{No-autoimmune protocol and transfer conditions}
\label{sec:transferprotocol}
To test whether related autoimmune data matters, a second protocol also removes TxGNN's autoimmune area (all descendants of DOID:417 in the Human Disease Ontology, mapped to PrimeKG through MONDO cross-references; 70 nodes)~\cite{huang2024txgnn} and the families of all nine autoimmune diseases studied here, 123 nodes in total. Its 21 held-out area diseases with indications form a further test set. The \emph{transfer curve} uses a fixed test set $E$: the 12 area diseases with indications that are neither targets nor RA/SLE/MS relatives. $E$, the targets and the validation diseases are held out in every condition; only the training diseases change:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A}: the RA/SLE/MS families (11 diseases, 161 indications);
\item \textbf{A$'$}: three sets of 11 non-autoimmune diseases, each matched to A disease by disease on number of indications;
\item \textbf{D}: all \TrDNTrain{} non-autoimmune diseases;
\item \textbf{C$_E$}: all \TrCENTrain{} diseases except $E$.
\end{itemize}
A vs.\ A$'$ isolates disease type at equal data size; C$_E$ vs.\ D measures autoimmune data on top of broad data. Since the message graph contains only training diseases' drug edges, each condition also fixes what drug knowledge the model sees. An ``all autoimmune'' condition is impossible: beyond RA/SLE/MS, PrimeKG's autoimmune diseases are the evaluation area itself.

\subsection{Few-shot evaluation}
\label{sec:fewshotprotocol}
For each test disease with at least four indications (\FsN{} of 68) we draw a random order of its indications (\FsDraws{} draws). The first $k_{\max}=3$ drugs form the reveal pool, and the remaining indications are the evaluation positives, identical for every $k$, so $k=0,\dots,3$ are compared on the same targets. With $k$ revealed drugs, the trained model receives the $k$ (disease, drug) indication edges in its message graph for this query only; it is \emph{not} retrained. Revealed drugs are removed from the ranking. Baselines see the same drugs: \emph{co-indication}, an item-based collaborative filter~\cite{sarwar2001itemcf} that scores a drug by the cosine similarity of its indication profile over training diseases to those of the revealed drugs; \emph{target similarity}, the Jaccard similarity of protein-target sets; disease $k$NN, which ignores the revealed drugs; and disease $k$NN plus co-indication. We also fuse the model with co-indication by reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF, constant 60)~\cite{cormack2009rrf}, which has no tuned weight.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\sysname scores a query (disease $u$, relation \emph{indication}) against every drug with two channels whose logits are added: a path channel that follows BioPathNet's NBFNet design and a prior channel of four leakage-free features. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture with all layer sizes; the subsections below describe each part.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{fig_architecture.pdf}
\caption{\sysname architecture. A query starts as an indicator vector on disease $u$. Six NBF layers pass DistMult messages over a message graph that never contains the drug edges of $u$'s family (dashed arrow: one sparse matrix $A_r$ per relation). An MLP reads each drug's layer states into a path score, a second MLP turns four leakage-free priors into a prior score, and their sum is ranked over all 7,957 drugs.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Path channel}
For a query $(u, r_q)$, NBFNet~\cite{zhu2021nbfnet} computes a representation of every node $v$ that summarises all paths from $u$ to $v$. Node states start from an indicator boundary condition, $h^{(0)}_v=\mathbf{1}[v=u]\,q$, with $q=\mathrm{emb}(r_q)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, and each of $L$ layers aggregates relation-specific messages:
\begin{align}
m^{(t)}_v &= \frac{1}{\deg v}\sum_{(x,r,v)\in\mathcal{E}} h^{(t-1)}_x \odot w^{(t)}_{r_q,r} \;+\; \mathbf{1}[v=u]\,q,\label{eq:msg}\\
h^{(t)}_v &= h^{(t-1)}_v + \mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\mathrm{LN}\big(W^{(t)}\big[h^{(t-1)}_v;\, m^{(t)}_v;\, a_v\, m^{(t)}_v\big]\big)\Big).\label{eq:upd}
\end{align}
Here $w^{(t)}_{r_q,r}$ are query-dependent DistMult relation vectors~\cite{yang2015distmult}, $a_v=\log(\deg v+1)/\overline{\log(\deg+1)}$ is a PNA-style degree amplifier~\cite{corso2020pna} and LN is layer normalisation. The path logit of drug $d$ is $s_\text{path}(u,d)=\mathrm{MLP}([h^{(1)}_d;\dots;h^{(L)}_d;q])$. Because propagation starts at $u$, one forward pass scores all 7,957 drugs, and a disease whose drugs were never seen is handled like any other node.

\subsection{Memory-efficient full-graph propagation}
NBFNet implementations usually materialise per-edge messages, which needs $O(|\mathcal{E}|\,B\,d)$ memory for $B$ queries: 4\,M edges $\times$ 8 queries $\times$ 32 dimensions per layer here. We write~\eqref{eq:msg} as $\sum_r (A_r H)\odot w_r$ over 60 sparse adjacency matrices $A_r$. A custom autograd function stores only $H$ and recomputes $A_r H$ in the backward pass, reducing activation memory to $O(|\mathcal{V}|\,B\,d)$. The graph takes 0.32\,GB; training with $B=8$ peaks at 5.8\,GB and takes about 8.5\,s per step on an NVIDIA T4, and the gradients pass a finite-difference check.

\subsection{Prior channel}
The prior channel adds four features $\phi(u,d)$ per pair: (i)~popularity, the number of training diseases indicated for $d$; (ii)~a disease-$k$NN score, $\sum_{u'\in\mathcal{N}_k(u)}\mathrm{sim}(u,u')\,y_{u'd}$ with $k=20$ and cosine similarity of TF--IDF-weighted disease--protein and disease--phenotype profiles; (iii)~the number of $d$'s protein targets associated with $u$; and (iv)~the frequency of $d$ among autoimmune training diseases. Each feature is computed with $u$'s family excluded, identically in training and testing. A two-layer MLP $g$ maps $\phi$ to a logit: $s(u,d)=s_\text{path}(u,d)+g(\phi(u,d))$.

\subsection{Objective}
Let $P_u$ be the training indications of $u$. We minimise a multi-positive softmax cross-entropy over all candidates,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(u)=-\frac{1}{|P_u|}\sum_{d\in P_u}\log\frac{\exp s(u,d)}{\sum_{d'}\exp s(u,d')},
\end{equation}
so every non-indicated drug acts as a negative and no negative sampling is needed. NBFNet's self-adversarial binary cross-entropy is evaluated as an ablation.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Baselines}
All baselines use the same protocol and family exclusions. \emph{Random}; \emph{drug degree}; \emph{popularity} (number of training diseases treated); \emph{autoimmune frequency}; \emph{shared targets} (drug targets among the disease's genes); and \emph{disease $k$NN}, the drugs of the 20 most similar training diseases weighted by similarity, a transparent form of guilt by association~\cite{bang2023dreamwalk}. \emph{KG embeddings}: TransE, DistMult and RotatE~\cite{bordes2013transe,yang2015distmult,sun2019rotate}, trained on exactly the message graph of the test split (all context edges plus the drug--disease edges of training diseases), with self-adversarial negative sampling~\cite{sun2019rotate} (256 negatives shared within each batch of 1,024 triples), 128 real dimensions per entity, Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$), 20 epochs and the epoch chosen by validation MRR (checked every 4 epochs; it peaked between epochs 12 and 20 and never exceeded 0.06); a test disease keeps its context edges, so it has an embedding even without drug edges. These settings were fixed in advance (\texttt{bpp/kge.py}). TxGNN is compared on its own split (Section~\ref{sec:txgnn}).

\subsection{Metrics}
For disease $u$ with positives $P_u$, each positive $d$ is ranked among all drugs after removing the other positives (filtered rank~\cite{bordes2013transe}):
\begin{align}
\mathrm{MRR}_u&=\frac{1}{|P_u|}\sum_{d\in P_u}\frac{1}{\mathrm{rank}_u(d)},\\
\mathrm{H@}k_u&=\frac{1}{|P_u|}\sum_{d\in P_u}\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{rank}_u(d)\le k],
\end{align}
macro-averaged over diseases~\cite{voorhees1999trec}. We also report Any@10, the share of diseases with at least one known drug in the top 10; AUROC and AUPRC over all drugs; AUROC against \emph{hard} negatives (drugs indicated for other autoimmune diseases); and AUROC against \emph{degree-matched} negatives. With about 7 positives among 7,957 drugs, random ranking gives MRR and AUPRC of about 0.001 and Any@10 of \RandExpAnyTenTest\%. AUROC hardly reflects this imbalance~\cite{saito2015prc}, so it is not a headline metric (Section~\ref{sec:auroc}). MRR is easiest to read through ranks: \sysname's test MRR of \TestMrr{} corresponds to a median known-drug rank of \MedRankTest, with \RankOneShareTest\% of known drugs ranked first.

\subsection{Statistics and training}
Every comparison uses per-disease metrics averaged over seeds and then over diseases. We report seed mean $\pm$ sd, percentile bootstrap 95\% CIs over diseases (1,000 resamples)~\cite{efron1993bootstrap} and, for comparisons, the paired bootstrap CI of the mean difference (5,000 resamples) with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945}. A difference is significant only if both agree at the 5\% level. Training: hidden size 32, 6 layers, MLP hidden size 64, dropout 0.1, Adam with learning rate $5\times10^{-3}$, 8 query diseases per batch, gradient clipping at 1.0, at most 20 epochs, early stopping on validation MRR (patience 5--6); 68,226 parameters; seeds 0--2. Transfer runs train on 11 diseases (2 steps per epoch) and may run 60 epochs with patience 10. Settings were fixed in advance, not tuned. Runs used two NVIDIA T4 GPUs on Kaggle.

\textbf{Why AUROC is not the headline metric.}
\label{sec:auroc}
Baselines without learning reach validation AUROC 0.78--0.86, and popularity \PopValidAuroc, yet their MRR is below 0.06. With thousands of negatives per positive, AUROC rewards placing positives above the long tail of irrelevant drugs and says little about the top of the list, which is what a clinician reads. The same effect let a degree heuristic reach AUROC 0.972 in the audit.

\subsection{Few-shot: a few known drugs}
\label{sec:fewshot}
Table~\ref{tab:fewshot} shows what a few known drugs add on the \FsN{} test diseases with at least four indications. Revealed as graph edges, they help the zero-shot-trained model without retraining: MRR rises from \FsBppZeroMrr{} to \FsBppOneMrr, \FsBppTwoMrr{} and \FsBppThreeMrr{} for one, two and three revealed drugs, a significant gain for every $k$ (at $k=3$: \FsBppGainThree, CI \FsBppGainLoThree{} to \FsBppGainHiThree; $p$\,\FsBppGainPThree). The model stays significantly ahead of disease $k$NN at every $k$ ($p$\,\FsPBppKnnThree{} at $k=3$). Co-indication filtering, which recommends drugs indicated for the same training diseases as the revealed ones, catches up quickly: the model's lead is not significant from $k=1$ (\FsDiffBppCoindOne, $p$\,\FsPBppCoindOne), and at $k=3$ the two are tied (\FsDiffBppCoindThree, $p$\,\FsPBppCoindThree). Once some drugs are known, much of the signal is therefore ``drugs used together'', which collaborative filtering captures directly, whereas the model, trained only zero-shot, sees the revealed drugs merely as extra edges. The two views are complementary. Reciprocal-rank fusion of the model with co-indication gives the highest MRR at every $k\geq1$ (\FsRrfOneMrr, \FsRrfTwoMrr, \FsRrfThreeMrr), significantly above the model alone at $k=3$ ($p$\,\FsPBppRrfThree), and \FsDiffRrfKnnCoindThree{} above the best baseline, disease $k$NN plus co-indication, at $k=3$ (CI \FsDiffLoRrfKnnCoindThree{} to \FsDiffHiRrfKnnCoindThree, $p$\,\FsPRrfKnnCoindThree), a margin that is not significant with \FsN{} diseases. Target similarity is weak at every $k$. Training the model with randomly revealed drugs is the natural next step.

\label{sec:aim3}

\subsection{Head-to-head with TxGNN on its own split}
\label{sec:txgnn}
We ran both models on TxGNN's autoimmune disease-area split, generated by TxGNN's code (commit \texttt{0e0203c}; Table~\ref{tab:txgnn}). Its random test edges are drawn without a seed, so we fixed the seed and the draw differs from the published runs. The test set is the \TxhN{} held-out area diseases with indications (\TxhPairs{} pairs). We converted the split so that our model never sees an edge that TxGNN does not train on: the \TxhDroppedEdges{} random test edges of \TxhDroppedDis{} other diseases are removed from our graph and labels, and area diseases are held out with their families. \sysname trains on \TxhTrainDis{} diseases (\TxhValidDis{} more for early stopping), while TxGNN learns from all \TxhNonTestDis{} non-test diseases with indications, so our model still sees less. TxGNN used its reproduction settings (one pre-training epoch, 500 fine-tuning epochs); each model ran \TxhSeedsTx{} seeds. Our implementation of TxGNN's protocol reproduces TxGNN's own evaluation exactly for every seed.

Over all 7,957 drugs, \sysname is significantly better: MRR \TxhOursMrr{} against \TxhTxMrr{} (\TxhDiffFullMrr, CI \TxhDiffLoFullMrr{} to \TxhDiffHiFullMrr, $p$\,\TxhPwilFullMrr), H@100 \TxhOursHitsHund{} against \TxhTxHitsHund{} ($p$\,\TxhPwilFullHitsHund) and AUROC \TxhOursFullAuroc{} against \TxhTxFullAuroc{} ($p$\,\TxhPwilFullAuroc). \section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Path-based zero-shot transfer works for rare autoimmune diseases that a model has never seen. With a handful of training diseases, related autoimmune diseases are the most useful source; with the full graph, adding the autoimmune diseases does not help and even lowers accuracy on held-out autoimmune diseases, so broad pharmacology and biology are the better source. Under a strict-family protocol with full ranking, \sysname outperforms every baseline evaluated on the same split, including KG embeddings, and on TxGNN's own split it ranks all drugs significantly better than TxGNN. When a few drugs of a disease are known, the model improves without retraining, but co-indication filtering catches up after one or two drugs; fusing the two gives the best rankings, although not significantly better than the strongest baseline. Path reasoning drives accuracy, the prior channel adds stability, and most of PrimeKG's edges are redundant for this task.

\textbf{How the numbers compare.} Our MRRs lie within the range BioPathNet reports on TxGNN's disease areas~\cite{hu2026biopathnet}, but those splits remove disease areas and most molecular edges whereas we hold out families and keep molecular context, so we compare with TxGNN only on a shared split. The earlier pipeline's AUPRC of 0.999 measured an easier task, separating graph edges from random pairs; near-perfect zero-shot numbers should be met with scepticism.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~The targets have 1--24 labelled drugs, so per-target metrics are noisy and can be lower bounds (AIH). (ii)~$E$ has 12 diseases and the TxGNN comparison \TxhN; BioPathNet itself was not re-run on a shared split. (iii)~Corticosteroids and antibiotics dominate the lists, partly a popularity bias. (iv)~PrimeKG labels are incomplete, so some true candidates count as false positives. (v)~The few-shot model is not trained with revealed drugs. (vi)~Literature labels were AI-assisted and checked against abstracts, but no clinician has reviewed the candidates; the rankings are hypotheses, not treatment recommendations. Future work: few-shot training, BioPathNet and TxGNN on further shared splits, and clinician review with the prepared sheet.
